## Part 1 — Expand Existing Buried Form Pages

### Task 1 — Expand: /resources/form-5471/

**Current status:** 5 impressions, position 58. Low volume but very niche — the people searching this are exactly Vantis's client type.

**Target queries:** "form 5471," "form 5471 filing requirements," "form 5471 categories of filers," "us shareholder foreign corporation"

**What to add:**
- What Form 5471 is: information return required when a US person is an officer, director, or shareholder in a foreign corporation
- The five categories of filers (Categories 1–5) — explained in plain English, not IRS code language
  - Category 1: 10% US shareholders of foreign corporations that are SFCs (specified foreign corps)
  - Category 2: officers and directors of foreign corps when a US person acquires 10%+
  - Category 3: US persons who acquire or dispose of 10%+ of a foreign corp
  - Category 4: US persons who are officers or directors of CFC
  - Category 5: 10%+ US shareholders of a CFC
- What a CFC (Controlled Foreign Corporation) is and why it triggers enhanced reporting
- The schedules: what each schedule reports (Schedule C = income statement, Schedule E = taxes paid, Schedule J = earnings and profits, Schedule O = acquisitions/dispositions)
- GILTI (Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income): when a CFC has income, the US shareholder may owe tax even without a distribution — brief explanation with a link to /services/international-tax/
- Penalties for failure to file: $10,000 per year, per form
- Common situation: US citizen or green card holder who owns shares in a foreign family business
- Common situation: US person who set up a foreign holding company for their Latin American operations
- Internal links to: /resources/form-8621/, /resources/foreign-gifts-and-inheritances-form-3520/, /services/international-tax/foreign-business-and-investors/

### Task 2 — Expand: /resources/form-8832-entity-classification-elections/

**Current status:** 5 impressions, position 54. Buried. Pair this page tightly with the foreign-owned LLC content — that's the main use case.

**Target queries:** "form 8832 entity classification," "check the box election," "form 8832 foreign entity," "disregarded entity election"

**What to add:**
- What Form 8832 is: the "check-the-box" election that lets eligible entities choose how they're classified for US federal tax purposes
- Default classifications: what a single-member LLC is treated as by default (disregarded entity), what a multi-member LLC is treated as (partnership), what a foreign eligible entity defaults to
- When to use Form 8832: converting a disregarded entity to a corporation, converting a partnership to a corporation, electing out of per-se corporate status for a foreign entity
- The foreign entity use case: a foreign corporation with a single US owner can elect to be treated as a disregarded entity — eliminating Form 5471 filing requirements but replacing it with Form 8858
- Retroactive elections: Form 8832 can be filed retroactively up to 75 days — a useful planning tool
- The late election relief procedure
- Form 8832 vs. Form 2553: 8832 is for changing classification (including to corporation), 2553 is for electing S-corp status once already classified as a corporation
- Interaction with Form 5472: a single-member foreign-owned LLC that is a disregarded entity still has a Form 5472 filing requirement
- Common mistake: using Form 8832 to try to elect S-corp status (you can't — that requires Form 2553 after the entity is already treated as a corporation)
- Internal links to: /resources/smllc-5472/, /resources/foreign-owned-us-llc-ecommerce-sellers/, /resources/form-5472-vs-1120-f-vs-us-subsidiary/

## Part 2 — New International Form Pages

### New Page: /resources/form-1040-nr/

**Target queries:** "form 1040-nr," "1040nr nonresident alien," "who must file 1040-nr," "form 1040-nr vs 1040"

**Angle:** Any foreign national with US-source income — rental income, wages, business income, investment income — may need to file a 1040-NR. This is a foundational page for Vantis's entire foreign client base.

**Must cover:**
- What Form 1040-NR is: the US income tax return for nonresident aliens
- Who must file: nonresident aliens with US-source income, foreign nationals who were in the US and earned wages, foreign investors with US rental income or investment income
- Resident vs. nonresident alien: the substantial presence test and the green card test — brief overview with link to /resources/physical-presence-test-us-expats/
- Dual-status year: what happens in the year you arrive in or depart the US
- What income is reportable on a 1040-NR: effectively connected income (ECI) vs. fixed and determinable annual or periodic income (FDAP)
- FDAP income: dividends, interest, rents, royalties — subject to 30% withholding (or treaty rate) rather than progressive rates
- ECI: business income, wages, rental income under the Section 871(d) election — subject to the same rates as US residents
- The Section 871(d) election: foreign investors with US rental property can elect to treat rental income as ECI, allowing deduction of expenses
- ITIN requirement: nonresident aliens without an SSN need an ITIN to file — link to /resources/itin-application-form-w-7/
- Treaty benefits: how tax treaties can reduce or eliminate US tax on certain types of income
- Deductions and credits available on the 1040-NR vs. what's not available (no standard deduction, limited itemized deductions)
- Filing deadline: same as 1040 if wages subject to withholding, June 15 if not
- Internal links to: /resources/physical-presence-test-us-expats/, /resources/foreign-owned-us-rental-property/, /resources/itin-application-form-w-7/, /resources/form-5472-vs-1120-f-vs-us-subsidiary/

**Meta description:** Nonresident aliens with US rental income, wages, or investment income must file Form 1040-NR. Here's who qualifies as a nonresident, what income is taxable, and how treaty benefits apply.

### New Page: /resources/form-926/

**Target queries:** "form 926," "form 926 transfer to foreign corporation," "irs form 926 filing requirement," "transfer property foreign corporation"

**Angle:** US persons who transfer property — cash, appreciated assets, intellectual property — to a foreign corporation must report it on Form 926. Missing this creates large penalties. Very relevant for clients restructuring their international business holdings.

**Must cover:**
- What Form 926 is: required when a US person transfers property to a foreign corporation in exchange for stock or as a contribution to capital
- Who must file: US persons (individuals, corporations, trusts, partnerships) who transfer property to a foreign corporation
- What counts as a "transfer": cash contributions, property contributions, exchanges under Section 351 (tax-free incorporation)
- Filing thresholds: Form 926 is required if the transfer is more than $100,000 in a 12-month period, or if the transferor owns 10%+ of the foreign corporation after the transfer
- The Section 367 toll charge: transfers to foreign corporations that would normally be tax-free under Section 351 may trigger gain recognition under Section 367
- Gain recognition agreements (GRAs): how US persons can defer gain on a transfer to a foreign corp by entering into a GRA with the IRS
- Transfers of intangibles: Section 367(d) — special rules apply when transferring intellectual property to a foreign corp
- Penalties for failure to file: 10% of the value of the transferred property, up to $100,000 (with no cap if the failure was intentional)
- Common situation: US business owner setting up a foreign holding structure to hold non-US operations
- Common situation: US person contributing cash to fund a foreign subsidiary
- Internal links to: /resources/form-5471/, /services/international-tax/foreign-business-and-investors/

**Meta title:** Form 926: Reporting Transfers of Property to a Foreign Corporation | Vantis CPA
**Meta description:** US persons who transfer property or cash to a foreign corporation must file Form 926. Here's when it's required, how Section 367 affects tax-free transfers, and the penalties for missing it.

### New Page: /resources/form-w8ben/

**Target queries:** "form w-8ben," "w8ben certificate of foreign status," "w-8ben vs w-8ben-e," "how to fill out w-8ben"

**Angle:** Every foreign contractor, vendor, or investor dealing with a US payer gets asked for a W-8BEN. Most don't know what it is, why the US company wants it, or what the difference is between W-8BEN and W-8BEN-E. This is one of the most common questions foreign clients have.

**Must cover:**
- What Form W-8BEN is: a certificate of foreign status that tells a US payer you are not a US person and not subject to the same withholding rules
- W-8BEN vs. W-8BEN-E: W-8BEN is for foreign individuals, W-8BEN-E is for foreign entities
- Why US companies request it: to document that withholding at 30% applies (or a reduced treaty rate), and to protect themselves from backup withholding liability
- The standard withholding rate: 30% on FDAP income paid to foreign persons — dividends, interest, royalties, rents, service income if US-sourced
- Claiming a treaty rate: how to complete Part II of the W-8BEN to claim a reduced treaty rate
- Which countries have withholding rate treaties with the US (mention the key Latin American ones and compare — Venezuela and Peru have no treaty, Mexico has a comprehensive treaty)
- How long the W-8BEN is valid: generally 3 years unless there's a change in circumstances
- Who does NOT use a W-8BEN: foreign persons with ECI (business income effectively connected to the US) — they use W-8ECI instead
- The W-8BEN-E: for foreign entities, additional sections for FATCA status — briefly explain the common FATCA categories
- Common mistake: a foreign-owned US LLC (which is a US person for tax purposes) should NOT give a W-8BEN — it should give a W-9
- Internal links to: /resources/ein-without-ssn-foreign-owners/, /resources/form-8938-fatca/, /resources/foreign-owned-us-llc-ecommerce-sellers/

**Meta title:** Form W-8BEN and W-8BEN-E: The Foreign Person's Certificate of Status | Vantis CPA
**Meta description:** US companies request Form W-8BEN to confirm you're a foreign person and determine the correct withholding rate. Here's what it is, how to claim a treaty rate, and the difference between W-8BEN and W-8BEN-E.

### New Page: /resources/form-8865/

**Target queries:** "form 8865," "form 8865 foreign partnership," "us person foreign partnership reporting," "irs form 8865 filing requirement"

**Angle:** US persons with interests in foreign partnerships — a common structure for Latin American business owners who operate partly in the US and partly abroad — must file Form 8865. Very few people know this exists.

**Must cover:**
- What Form 8865 is: information return for US persons with interests in foreign partnerships
- Who must file — the four categories:
  - Category 1: US person who controlled a foreign partnership (more than 50% interest)
  - Category 2: US person with 10%+ interest in a foreign partnership that had a Category 1 filer who was not a US person
  - Category 3: US person who contributed property to a foreign partnership (similar to Form 926 for corporations)
  - Category 4: US person who had a reportable event (acquisition, disposition, change in proportional interest)
- What qualifies as a "foreign partnership": any partnership not organized under US law, including Latin American sociedad anónima structures that are classified as partnerships for US tax purposes
- The check-the-box connection: Form 8832 can affect whether a foreign entity is classified as a partnership or corporation — mention the interaction
- PFIC exposure through foreign partnerships: if the foreign partnership holds foreign investment funds, the US partner may have PFIC exposure
- GILTI: if the foreign partnership owns a CFC, the US partner's allocable share of GILTI flows through
- Penalties for failure to file: $10,000 per year, per form
- Common situation: US resident with a business interest in a Latin American partnership (sociedad de responsabilidad limitada, sociedad anónima)
- Internal links to: /resources/form-5471/, /resources/pfic-tax-rules/, /services/international-tax/foreign-business-and-investors/

**Meta title:** Form 8865: US Persons with Interests in Foreign Partnerships | Vantis CPA
**Meta description:** US persons with interests in foreign partnerships — including Latin American business structures — must file Form 8865. Here's who must file, the four categories, and the $10,000 penalty for missing it.

### New Page: /resources/form-1116-foreign-tax-credit/

**Target queries:** "form 1116 foreign tax credit," "how to claim foreign tax credit," "form 1116 vs feie," "foreign tax credit us expats"

**Note:** "Foreign tax credit" is dominated by TurboTax and major tax sites. Do NOT try to rank for the generic query. Build this around the niche angles: expats in high-tax countries, Latin American business owners paying taxes abroad, and the FEIE vs. FTC decision.

**Angle:** The Foreign Tax Credit is often the better choice over the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion for expats in high-tax countries — but the calculation is complicated and the passive vs. general basket rules catch most people off guard.

**Must cover:**
- What Form 1116 is: the form used to claim a credit against US tax for foreign income taxes paid or accrued
- Who benefits most: expats and dual residents in countries with higher tax rates than the US (UK, Germany, Brazil, Canada, Israel, Australia)
- The two income baskets: general income (wages, business income) and passive income (dividends, interest, capital gains) — why the baskets matter and how they limit the credit
- The credit vs. exclusion decision: when Form 1116 + Foreign Tax Credit beats the FEIE (link to /resources/feie-vs-ftc/)
- Carryover and carryback: unused foreign tax credits can carry back 1 year and carry forward 10 years
- High-tax kickout: when passive income is taxed at a high foreign rate, it gets reclassified as general income
- The simplified limitation election for individuals with ≤$300 ($600 MFJ) in passive foreign taxes
- Latin American context: Brazil, Colombia, Argentina, and Mexico all have high effective tax rates — US residents with income from these countries often benefit from the FTC over the FEIE
- Treaty interaction: tax treaties can reduce foreign withholding to a rate below the US rate, which reduces the available credit
- Self-employed expats: the SE tax issue — FEIE doesn't eliminate SE tax, but the FTC doesn't reduce it either. The section 911(d)(6) rule.
- Internal links to: /resources/feie-vs-ftc/, /resources/form-2555-feie/, /resources/physical-presence-test-us-expats/, /resources/us-tax-for-brazilians/
