[Form 5471](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-5471) is the information return U.S. persons (citizens, residents, domestic corporations, partnerships, and trusts) file when they have certain ownership of, or control over, a foreign corporation. It is one of the most demanding forms in the tax code: a main form plus more than a dozen schedules that can require full financial statements, earnings and profits computations, and anti-deferral calculations for a company that keeps its books in another country, another currency, and another accounting standard. It is also the form the IRS uses to police the CFC rules, which can tax U.S. shareholders on foreign corporate profits that were never distributed.

{{< callout title="Why the form gets expensive fast" tone="warning" >}}
The tax is not always the first problem. The first problem is usually that the records needed to complete Form 5471 were never gathered in a usable way during the year, and the second is that nobody realized the ownership thresholds had been crossed until years later.
{{< /callout >}}

## Who Must File: The Five Categories

The filing obligation depends on which "category" of filer you are, and the category determines how much of the form you complete. In plain English:

**Category 1** applies to 10% U.S. shareholders of certain "specified foreign corporations." It exists mainly because of the 2017 transition tax and today it mostly catches shareholders of foreign corporations that have a corporate U.S. shareholder but are not CFCs.

**Category 2** applies to U.S. citizens and residents who are **officers or directors** of a foreign corporation in which a U.S. person acquired a 10% stake during the year. You can owe this filing without owning a single share yourself.

**Category 3** is the **transaction category**: a U.S. person who acquires stock that puts them at or above 10%, acquires an additional 10%, disposes of stock that drops them below 10%, or becomes a U.S. person while already owning 10%. It captures the year something changed.

**Category 4** applies to a U.S. person who **controlled** the foreign corporation, generally more than 50% of vote or value, for at least 30 consecutive days during the year.

**Category 5** applies to **U.S. shareholders of a controlled foreign corporation (CFC)**: U.S. persons owning at least 10% of a foreign corporation that is more than 50% owned by such U.S. shareholders. Category 5 filers carry the heaviest burden, including the Subpart F, GILTI, and previously taxed earnings schedules.

Two things make the categories treacherous. First, **constructive ownership**: shares held by family members and related entities can be attributed to you, so a 6% direct stake can still produce a filing obligation. Second, the categories **overlap**, and a shareholder can be in more than one at once. The full breakdown, including the subcategories and the exceptions that can relieve duplicate filings, is in our guide to the [Form 5471 categories of filers](form-5471-categories-of-filers.md).

## What Is a CFC?

A **controlled foreign corporation** is a foreign corporation more than 50% owned (by vote or value) by U.S. shareholders who each own at least 10%. The definition sounds simple and is not: ownership is measured directly, indirectly through foreign entities, and constructively through attribution rules that reach family members and related companies. A family business abroad, a 50/50 joint venture with one other U.S. investor, or a startup incorporated overseas by U.S. founders can all be CFCs without anyone intending it.

CFC status is what converts Form 5471 from a disclosure exercise into a tax event, because it activates the anti-deferral rules described below. How the ownership tests and attribution rules actually work, and the planning options once you are caught, are covered in our guide to [controlled foreign corporations](controlled-foreign-corporation-cfc.md).

## What the Form Requires

Depending on filer category, Form 5471 may require:

- The foreign corporation's **income statement and balance sheet**, translated to U.S. dollars and U.S. accounting concepts
- **Earnings and profits (E&P)** computations, current and accumulated
- **Transactions between the corporation and its U.S. shareholders** and other related parties
- **Subpart F income** and **net CFC tested income (GILTI)** calculations
- Foreign taxes paid, organizational changes, and distributions

Each of those lives on its own schedule, lettered A through R, and which schedules you complete depends on your category. Our [schedule-by-schedule guide to Form 5471](form-5471-schedules.md) walks through what each one reports and where the real preparation work is.

## Subpart F and GILTI: The Tax That Rides Along

U.S. shareholders of CFCs must include certain types of the corporation's income in their own U.S. taxable income every year, regardless of whether anything was distributed. This "deemed inclusion" applies to:

- **Subpart F income:** passive income, certain related-party payments, and other categories defined under [IRC Sections 951 through 965](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/corporations/subpart-f-income)
- **GILTI, now renamed net CFC tested income (NCTI):** a broad minimum tax on CFC operating profits, introduced in 2017 and significantly reworked by the 2025 tax law for tax years beginning in 2026

For individual shareholders the default GILTI result is harsh: ordinary rates with no deduction and no corporate foreign tax credit. Elections exist that change the answer dramatically, including the Section 962 election and the high-tax exclusion. The regime, the 2026 changes, and the planning levers are covered in our guide to [GILTI and net CFC tested income](gilti-tax.md).

## Dormant Corporations: The Short-Form Option

A foreign corporation that conducted no business, had de minimis income and expenses (generally under $5,000), held limited assets (generally under $100,000), and made no distributions or ownership changes may qualify as a **dormant foreign corporation** under Rev. Proc. 92-70. For a dormant corporation, the filer completes only the identifying information on page one and labels the filing as a summary return. That is a real concession, but note what it is not: it is not an exemption. The form still has to be filed every year the corporation exists, and the $10,000 penalty applies to a skipped summary filing just as it does to a skipped full one.

## Penalties

The penalty for failure to file Form 5471 is **$10,000 per form, per year**, applied automatically in many cases. If the failure continues more than 90 days after the IRS mails a notice, continuation penalties of $10,000 per 30-day period accrue, up to an additional $50,000 per form. The IRS may also reduce your foreign tax credits by 10%.

Just as important: a missing Form 5471 keeps the **statute of limitations open on your entire income tax return**, not just the foreign items. A return filed fifteen years ago can still be examined today if the form was never attached.

## Common Situations We See

- **A share of the family business abroad.** A U.S. citizen or green card holder owns part of a corporation in Latin America, Europe, or Israel, often received by [gift or inheritance from foreign relatives](foreign-gifts-and-inheritances-form-3520.md). Attribution among family members frequently makes the company a CFC without anyone noticing.
- **A founder who incorporated overseas.** U.S. founders form a foreign company for a foreign market, or keep their original company when they move to the U.S. The company is usually a CFC from day one.
- **An entrepreneur relocating to the U.S.** A business owner becomes a U.S. resident while owning a foreign corporation. The filing obligations begin with residency, which is why [pre-immigration planning](pre-immigration-planning.md) before the residency start date matters so much.
- **A holding company that seemed harmless.** A foreign holding entity set up years ago for real estate or investments, dormant or nearly so, quietly generating a $10,000-per-year exposure.

## Can You Get Out of Form 5471?

Sometimes the structure can be simplified. A foreign entity with a single U.S. owner can often elect disregarded status with a [check-the-box election on Form 8832](form-8832-entity-classification-elections.md), which switches off the CFC rules and replaces Form 5471 with the shorter [Form 8858](form-8858.md). Multi-owner foreign entities may be able to elect partnership status, moving to [Form 8865](form-8865.md) instead. Each election is a real tax event with real consequences (a deemed liquidation, in the conversion case), so the modeling has to come before the election. And if the entity genuinely serves no purpose, a clean liquidation can end the annual filings entirely, with one final form on the way out.
