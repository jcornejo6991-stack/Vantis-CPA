The controlled foreign corporation rules answer a question the U.S. tax system has wrestled with since 1962: if U.S. taxpayers can park income inside a foreign corporation the IRS cannot tax directly, what stops everyone from doing exactly that? The answer is that once U.S. ownership crosses a control threshold, the corporation's income stops waiting politely to be distributed and starts flowing through to its U.S. shareholders each year, on their returns, at their rates. Whether that describes your foreign company turns entirely on ownership math, and the math counts more than what is in your own name.

{{< pullquote attribution="The core idea" >}}
A CFC is a foreign corporation the U.S. has decided to treat, for its controlling American owners, as too American to leave alone.
{{< /pullquote >}}

## The Two-Step Ownership Test

**Step one: find the U.S. shareholders.** A "U.S. shareholder" is a U.S. person (citizen, resident, domestic corporation, partnership, trust, or estate) owning **10% or more** of the foreign corporation's stock, by vote **or** value. Anyone below 10% is invisible to this test, no matter how American.

**Step two: add them up.** If U.S. shareholders together own **more than 50%** of the total vote or total value on any day of the corporation's year, the corporation is a CFC.

Both steps use three kinds of ownership:

- **Direct:** shares in your name
- **Indirect:** shares held through foreign entities, traced proportionally up to you
- **Constructive:** shares attributed from others, including family (spouse, children, grandchildren, parents) and entities you are related to

The constructive rules contain a carve-out that decides many family cases: **stock owned by a nonresident alien individual is generally not attributed to a U.S. family member**. The U.S.-citizen daughter holding 15% of the family company in Bogotá does not constructively own her Colombian father's 60%. But if the father takes a green card, his shares now count directly, and the company may become a CFC overnight. That single fact pattern is a large share of the [pre-immigration planning](pre-immigration-planning.md) work we do.

One historical footnote that still matters when reading older advice: from 2018 through 2025, "downward attribution" from foreign parents made many foreign subsidiaries of foreign groups technically CFCs. The 2025 tax law restored the old rule for tax years beginning in 2026 and replaced that regime with a narrower one for foreign-controlled shareholders, so structures diagnosed as CFCs during those years deserve a fresh look.

## How CFCs Happen By Accident

The label rarely arrives by design. The recurring patterns:

- **The wholly owned company.** Any foreign corporation 100% owned by one U.S. person is a CFC, full stop. Founders who incorporate abroad, and expats who form a local company for their business, are here whether or not they have heard the term. For an operating business abroad, comparing this structure against a disregarded entity reported on [Form 8858](form-8858.md) is usually the first planning conversation.
- **The family business with U.S. members.** Attribution among U.S. family members aggregates stakes that individually look safe.
- **The 50/50 deal that isn't.** Side letters, preferred rights, or value shifts push one side past half.
- **Immigration.** The owner moves to the U.S., and the company's status changes with the owner's, generating a Category 3 filing in year one and Category 5 every year after.

## What CFC Status Actually Triggers

**Annual income inclusions.** The corporation's 10% U.S. shareholders pick up two streams of income every year regardless of distributions: **Subpart F income** (passive and certain related-party income, taxed at the shareholder's ordinary rates) and **net CFC tested income**, the regime formerly called GILTI, which sweeps in most active operating profit. The mechanics, the 2026 changes, and the elections that soften the result have [their own guide](gilti-tax.md).

**Form 5471, Category 5.** The heaviest version of the form: financial statements, earnings and profits, related-party transactions, and the tracking schedules. See the [Form 5471 overview](form-5471.md) and the [schedule-by-schedule guide](form-5471-schedules.md).

**PTEP tracking.** Income taxed to shareholders before distribution creates **previously taxed earnings and profits**, which must be tracked so the eventual cash distribution is not taxed twice. Neglected PTEP records do not eliminate the relief; they just make it expensive to prove.

**Exit consequences.** Gain on selling CFC stock can be recharacterized as a **dividend under Section 1248** to the extent of the corporation's untaxed earnings, which changes rates, treaty results, and foreign tax credit outcomes on the way out.

**A different regime than PFIC.** CFC and [PFIC](pfic-tax-rules.md) are the two anti-deferral systems, and they aim at different targets: CFC status follows controlling U.S. ownership of any foreign corporation, while PFIC status follows passive-heavy foreign corporations regardless of U.S. control, with [Form 8621](form-8621.md) as its reporting arm. Minority investors in foreign funds worry about PFIC; controlling owners of foreign companies worry about CFC; unlucky structures qualify for both, where the CFC rules generally take priority for the overlapping shareholders.

## The Planning Levers

CFC status is manageable, and sometimes even fine, but only when it is planned rather than discovered:

1. **Entity classification.** A single-owner foreign entity can often [check the box on Form 8832](form-8832-entity-classification-elections.md) to be disregarded, exiting the CFC regime entirely in exchange for flow-through treatment and [Form 8858](form-8858.md). Multi-owner entities may prefer partnership classification and [Form 8865](form-8865.md). The election is a deemed liquidation with real tax consequences, so it is modeled first, elected second.
2. **The Section 962 election** lets individual shareholders be taxed on CFC inclusions as if a domestic corporation received them, unlocking the corporate deduction and foreign tax credits. Covered in the [GILTI guide](gilti-tax.md).
3. **The high-tax exclusion** can carve income taxed heavily abroad out of the inclusion system altogether.
4. **Ownership design before residency.** The most valuable window is before a foreign owner becomes a U.S. person, when restructuring is still free of U.S. consequences. That is [pre-immigration planning](pre-immigration-planning.md), and it is strictly a before-the-move exercise.
5. **Compensation planning.** Salary, management fees, and rents paid by the CFC to its owner are deductible against tested income and taxed once, which often beats leaving profit inside to be included and distributed.
